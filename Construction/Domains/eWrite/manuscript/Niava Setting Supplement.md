# Chapter 0: Using This Book as a Narrator

## Purpose of This Book

Use this book to:

- Inform the tone and world logic of each region  
- Reveal cultural truths slowly, through social contact  
- Interpret rituals, political shifts, and technologies in multiple ways

## When to Introduce Lore

Narrators (GMs) should only introduce facts from this document to players when it makes sense:

- Through direct NPC conversation  
- As environmental storytelling (architecture, banners, rituals)  
- After a successful **Lore Check** or ritual-specific test

**Note:** Difficulty should be guided using the core rulebook’s TV scale.

- Untrained checks: TVs 1–5  
- Trained or specialized knowledge: TVs 5–10  
- Tiered characters may apply different scales entirely

Do not state raw Target Values. Instead, describe checks as:

- “Challenging for a trained historian”  
- “Obscure knowledge even among priests”  
- “General public awareness—no check required”

## Describing Space and Detail

Use architectural and cultural cues generously:

- If a room is “10’x12’x21’”, consider why the ceiling is that high.

## About the Observers

Although unnamed, the in-world narrators are a collection of scholars from beyond the mists. They are:

- Non-interventionists, but reform-minded  
- Technologically advanced (augmented sight, linguistic tools)  
- Writing for a future where understanding might prevent further conflict

# Chapter I: Gazetteer

### As Recorded by the Panel of Observers from Beyond the Mists, Circa 58 Years Past

## I. The Continent: Trand

The landmass called **Trand**, meaning ground by its residents, is the largest landmass in the world. From it lives the largest empire, controlling the largest army.

### Key Characteristics

- **Size & Location**: Trand dominates the central sea-laced band of the world. It is bordered by shallow coastlines, the Great Lake inland, and mountain walls to the far southeast.  
- **Climatic Zones**: Temperate in the north and center; semi-arid deserts toward the southeastern reaches; jungles along southern tributary deltas.  
- **Notable Features**: The **Great Lake**, the **Games Arena City**, and the **Empire Roadway** (a vast, stone-lined thoroughfare between Zuteibar and the sea).

---

## II. Imperial Structures

### The Otsaiapian Empire

One of the oldest surviving trans-kingdom alliances, the **Otsaiapian Empire** occupies Trand’s northwest and dominates through layered bureaucracy, ceremonial governance, and overwhelming military presence.

#### Member Kingdoms:

- **Canicia** – A populous and disciplined kingdom; capital: **Trakasu**. Home to the **Games Training Complex** and the seat of logistical military operations.  
- **Gagia** – Culturally rich and spiritually divided; often at odds with eastern neighbors and internally strained by ancient ritual lineages.  
- **Coviaria** – Northernmost coastal kingdom, colder and seafaring. Maintains vital ports and shipwright enclaves.  
- **Former Tratian Empire** – Now a vassal territory, stripped of self-rule after historic wars. Its people are barred from the Games.

**Callout (GM Use):** The Games were originally created as a peacekeeping mechanism among the Otsaiapian kingdoms to resolve imperial succession. Players may learn this during cultural exchanges, contested political debates, or elite NPC monologues.

#### Empire Capital:

- **Zuteibar** – Geographically distant, a great port city on the shore of the empire. Many frontier problems are ignored until they escalate.

---

## III. Independent & Contested States

### Long-standing Independent Powers:

- **Republic of Coniaria** and **Principality of Nyria**: Bound by treaty, these two states maintain independent trade networks and political traditions.

### Ongoing Conflicts:

- **Goria vs. Gagia**, with interloping from **Monzangia**: While technically under Otsaiapian protection, the Empire's influence is weak this far southwest. Skirmishes and mercenary disruptions are frequent.

---

## IV. Transit & Strategic Zones

### **Bilda**

Once peripheral, now pivotal, **Bilda** is the crossroads of empire, war, and migration. Positioned just southeast of the Games Arena City (that's the name of the city), it feeds commerce through:

- **River barges (north-south)** toward Trakasu and empire heartlands  
- **Stone roads (east-west)** connecting rebel zones, Games complexes, and extraction hubs

**Callout (GM Use):** Players may begin in **Naliuq**, a hamlet on the outskirts of Bilda. They may know it as a farming village (if they’re from a larger city), a merchant quarter (if they’re familiar with the area and know no farmers live there, only merchants), or for the  **Locked Courtyard (quickstart module)**, 

**Naliuq** (Hamlet)  
A slum-to-be. Known for:

- Market stalls and fences (including **Kessa**, a merchant with rare wares)  
- Local chatter about new **rituals** spreading from the south

**Note (GM Use):** The players’ journey likely begins in Naliuq and quickly transitions to the **Games Training Barracks**, where **Ra** introduces them to the next phase.

### **Military Presence**

Just north of Bilda lies the continent’s **largest combined army**, jointly stationed by Canicia, Coviaria, and Otsaiapia. Intended as a deterrent force, its real purpose is less clear to locals.

### **Games Arena City**

Southwestern shore of the Great Lake.

- Exists solely to **host the Games**.  
- Also maintains a **port to Coviaria**, though rarely used for civilian trade.

**Callout (GM Use):** The Games combine gladiatorial spectacle, agility trials, bloodsport, and social prestige. Think live weapons, real stakes, and voluntary participants. The players may encounter political entanglements around these Games before ever attending them.

## V. Lonall – The Southern Enigma

Often dismissed as a backwater by imperial elites, **Lonall** is anything but. Nestled along the southern coast beyond the desert rise, Lonall is a constellation of fiercely independent city-states, whose tribal roots mask a startling truth: their society is among the most advanced in the known world.

### Regional Characteristics:

- **Governance**: Loosely allied city-states with ritual-based validation and rotating councils  
- **Cultural Backbone**: Communal rites, local sovereignty, and reverence for learned elders  
- **Technological Profile**: Far beyond most of Trand, though described by locals using spiritual or magical terms to avoid internal disruption

**Callout (GM Use):** Upon arrival in Lonall, players will begin to sense a dramatic shift in architecture, communication, and possibly even medicine or sensory devices. GMs should describe these shifts subtly—lens overlays, responsive infrastructure, or gesture-based tools—leaving open whether these are technological or magical.

### Major Cities:

- **Aber**: Former capital; known for royal abdication in favor of the **Bread Standard**. Site of the future **Dinner Party**.  
- **Banvenven**: Transitional city-state, de facto capital. Culturally torn but increasingly Bread-aligned.  
- **Durum, Avonnach, Mabergy, Hordh**: Early adopters of the Bread Standard. Tight-knit, inter-cooperative communities.

**Note (Lore Clue):** The King of Aber famously abdicated the throne after extensive consultation with **Valora**, leader of the **Collegium**—Lonall’ greatest archive of knowledge. Her death marks the current generation’s uncertain inheritance.

**Callout (GM Use):** Many wealthy Longcugan elites oppose the Bread Standard. Some are fleeing with their wealth, and players may encounter them during the Act I exodus. These encounters can foreshadow ideological conflict or offer material temptation.

### The Collegium

Arguably the most important intellectual institution in Niava, the **Collegium** houses generational wisdom, advanced scientific insights, and possibly knowledge from beyond the mists themselves. Long believed to be the origin of the rituals now spreading through Trand.

> **Fun Fact:** Some Longcugan cities describe subdermal interfaces, floating text, and augmented sight as “the seeing of truth,” often integrated with rituals.

# Chapter II: World Dynamics

This chapter outlines the tensions, transformations, and systemic patterns shaping Trand and its surrounding powers during the period of observation. These are not static societies but evolving ones—marked by migrations, ritual innovation, economic shifts, and the awakening of new ideologies such as the Bread Standard.

---

## I. Economic and Cultural Currents

### Ritual Dissemination

- The spread of the **rituals**—notably *The Silent Audience* and *The Naming*—follows social rather than military or trade lines.  
- Originating in **Lonall**, these practices carry memetic power, reshaping identity and political legitimacy.  
- In cities like **Bilda** and **Banvenven**, to know or perform these rituals marks one as modern, spiritual, or suspicious depending on the audience.

**Callout (GM Use):** Characters may encounter differing interpretations of the same ritual across city-states. Encourage players to ask: "Who benefits from this ritual's framing here?"

### The Bread Standard (BS)

- A revolutionary socio-economic framework emerging from **Aber**.  
- Promotes dignity, sufficiency, and community-led validation over profit or lineage.  
- Increasingly adopted by southern cities; fiercely resisted by northern elites.

**Note:** Players are not expected to understand or adopt the Bread Standard, nor read its constitution. These are in place and available for reference.

### Resistance and Flight

- Wealthy families in Lonall are attempting to liquidate or smuggle assets.  
- Some seek passage off-continent; others aim to undermine authorities.  
- The **Games** and imperial strongholds are common convergence points for political actors trying to reroute cultural tides.

---

## II. Military and Political Alignments

### The Great Standoff (North of the Lake)

- Joint forces of **Canicia**, **Coviaria**, and **Otsaiapia** maintain the continent’s largest peacetime army just beyond **Bilda**.  
- Nominally a show of unity, in practice a pressure valve barely containing rivalry.

### Games-Driven Influence

- The **Games Arena City** represents both sport and power transfer.  
- Winning the **Crown Acquisition Challenge** can elevate a person—or a kingdom—to rulership.  
- Citizens of the former **Tratian Empire** are barred from participation, a point of unrest.

**Callout (GM Use):** The Games serve as a dramatic backdrop for political scheming, physical trials, and social gambits. The players’ path through training, selection, and infiltration reflects broader power dynamics.

---

## III. Technological Disparity and Disguise

- While **Lonall** integrates technologies that border on post-modern (e.g., embedded displays, responsive architecture), the empire labels these systems as heretical or magical.

**Callout (GM Use):** Narrators may describe tech in sensory or poetic terms first, then allow players to interpret or misinterpret it. This mystery preserves wonder and control.

# Chapter III: The Plot

This chapter outlines the underlying dramatic engine of the first arc—not to be handed to players, but to be held in readiness by the Narrator. The players will arrive in the middle of this momentum, mistaken for contenders or assistants or simply thrown into the machinery of someone else's design. They are not destined to be heroes. But they may yet alter the ending, if they choose to entangle themselves.

In Act I, the players learn the basics of their job, to get the child into the games by obtaining a crown from a distant former monarch's possession. From there, they plan and journey to a port city to make their way to the foreign land.

A friendly but clueless courier named Ra gathers the players and brings them to a Games training camp outside Bilda, where chaos reigns. There, the players meet Russel, an overwhelmed Master-at-Arms, and a paranoid knight guarding an eight-year-old foreign royal heir—a child placed illegally into the imperial Games by shadowy powers. None fully understand the implications, but the child’s placement hinges on recovering a crown stolen generations ago and now kept in a distant, ideologically divergent land. The party is tasked with retrieving this Crown of Aber, leading them on a journey to the coast and beyond into Lonall, a territory structured by ritual and shaped by Bread Standard values.

In Act II, the players enter a strange land in which their presence is celebrated and helped to the point of gaining an audience with the former monarch at an important dinner party. The Act ends with the players acquiring the monarch's crown and return to Russel in the interim between Acts.

In Lonall, the players are met by a cautious interpreter and the sharp, watchful Radan, both of whom reveal that the land is neither backward nor barbaric—but instead, startlingly advanced in thought, technology, and spiritual cohesion. Through dialog, ceremony, and tension, the party must navigate whether to negotiate diplomatically or attempt a heist during a formal validator dinner where the crown is kept. Their hosts neither obstruct nor aid them, but observe closely what kind of guests they’ve invited in. The return journey to Canicia is not triumphant—it is fraught with philosophical weight, questions of legitimacy, and choices that may outlast any throne. The players now hold both a crown and a mirror, and must decide if the Games should continue, be subverted, or end altogether.

Act III is in a separate volume and about the games, the child's performance in them, and how the players become involved in a plot against the empire. A plot they may want to join.

## The beginning

The journey begins with Ra, a friendly courier who was given one job: gather fresh recruits for the Games. Ra has no sense for what makes a good candidate. He grabbed whoever was nearby and looked capable. In his mind, he is doing a great service, maybe even shaping future champions. He talks extensively during the journey to the barracks. He speculates about the Games, about glory, about how lucky the players are. He mentions someone important waiting for them, though he doesn’t know who. He mentions the Master-at-Arms, though he forgets the name. He means well. He likes the players. He wishes them well. Then he leaves, like a goldfish, and forgets they were ever his responsibility.

What the players walk into is not a trial, but a breakdown.

At the Games training compound just outside of Bilda, tensions are already high. Rival trainees are fighting. Weapons are drawn. Training orders are being ignored. It is into this chaos that the players arrive. The Master-at-Arms, Russel, is shouting to restore order, embarrassed that this display is occurring in front of strangers. He is large, bearded, and overwhelmed. Upright and earnest, but clearly drowning.

Russel has been handed an impossible task. His financier, a distant and powerful figure, has placed a child in his care. The child is eight years old, a royal heir of Gagia, noble in bearing, but completely foreign in language and culture. The child is accompanied by a knight-interpreter whose instructions are clear: the child must not be harmed, not even in training. Russel does not know why this child must be in the Games. He only knows that the financier’s fortunes depend on it, and by extension, so does his own. The stakes are unspoken, but they press on every conversation.

The child, for their part, does not yet speak for themselves. They are composed, alert, and used to command—but not by choice. The weight of inherited expectation hangs over them. They respond to instruction, observe carefully, and carry themselves as royalty, though they are only a child. Players who attempt to speak to them will be met first with frustration—the language barrier is real, and the knight does not interpret generously. The knight is protective, terse, and paranoid. They suspect everyone of being a threat. And perhaps they are not wrong.

Unknown to nearly everyone involved, a greater scheme is underway. The Governor of Lassiz, hundreds of miles away, has made a private bet: that he can obtain a crown before the next Games. If he wins, his proxy becomes eligible for the games. That proxy is this child. The bet is not made out of loyalty to Gagia, but as a political maneuver. The Governor hopes to shape the child into a puppet Emperor, one indebted to him forever. None of this is meant to be known to the players—not until the end of the campaign, or unless they dramatically and irreversibly change the situation.

The players, then, are not chosen. They are convenient. They may be ignored, tested, or conscripted into the drama. Or they may walk away. It is in the second act that the players meet a third option, rather than emperor or puppet, but a new way to be altogether.

This is the first real choice of the campaign: do they see what’s happening, and act? Or do they keep their heads down and train? Do they help the child? Undermine them? Befriend them? Protect them? Do they compete in the Games themselves? Every route opens a different door.

Narrators should pose these dilemmas gently, through environment and encounter. Let the world show its fractures. Let Russel explain too little. Let the knight bristle. Let the child watch in silence. And let the players decide who their characters are becoming.

In Act I, players discover their purpose in obtaining the crown of Aber, and travel through their own feudal world toward the continent of Lonall. In Act II, characters continue their journey and form a heist plan to steal the Crown of Aber while undercover as attendees. In Act III, characters return to Canicia and face the Games that ensure the succession of emperors.

# Chapter IV: The Walkthrough Begins

This chapter  gives guidance for the Narrator to move players through the early arc of the campaign, allowing space for their choices to shape events while gradually unfolding the larger world. Each location and scene is meant to feel alive, not reactive, full of people and pressures that keep going whether the players participate or not. It resumes where the Quickstart leaves off, with Ra having delivered the party to Russel at the training grounds where there is chaos.

## Arrival at the Training Grounds

Russel does his best to regain control after the players arrive. There is shouting, sparring, and ego clashing among his existing trainees when they enter. It is an embarrassment he did not intend for them to see. Once he calms things down, he turns his attention to the new arrivals with a sort of desperate hope. He doesn't know why Ra brought them in particular, but if they are willing to help, then perhaps they are exactly who he needed.

He offers them room and board in the barracks, a place among the cohort, and what little training he can spare over the next few days. In exchange, he asks them to undertake an important journey—one he has not yet fully explained. His hope is to send them to Hervia within a week. From there, they may secure passage to Taurion and then to Aberghe, the doorstep of the Lonall continent. What waits beyond is unclear even to him. But he has orders. And those orders include moving people.

This initial week offers players their first breath. They can train, spar, tinker, rest, and ask questions. This is the perfect time to introduce systems of downtime: crafting, repairing, or earning money. It is a taste of rhythm before the tide changes. Let players experience safety and curiosity here, but allow the tensions of the place to remain present. Trainees whisper. The knight watches. The child appears and vanishes unpredictably playing tag with the other children in the structure.

Whether the players notice the child’s importance depends entirely on them. Some may see nothing but a quiet observer in regal clothes. Others may piece together the inconsistencies: the overprotective guard, the deference some give in whispers, the lack of discipline in sparring around him. There is no right time to reveal the truth. Only moments where something doesn’t add up.

Soon, an announcement spreads through the barracks and beyond. There will be celebratory Games in honor of the Emperor’s 50th year of rule. Rumors follow: that he will name an heir. The traditional rules may change. The Games are no longer just a sport. (Six years down the line, there is time, but the child needs to qualify before beginning to train).

Russel becomes more urgent. Whatever the players are doing, he begins pressing them to leave. Hervia awaits. There, they will meet contacts, merchants, scholars, maybe spies, who know how to get them to Taurion and on to Lonall. But he cannot make them go. He only tells them he must send someone.

On the road, stories await. The child may join them. Or not. The knight may warn them. Or vanish. The decisions of the players in this short training phase will shape who follows, who listens, and who trusts them. And the further they travel, the more the dinner party becomes real, not as a goal, but as a whisper. A table is being set.

# Chapter V: NPC Reference

## Russel, Master-at-Arms

**Name**: Russel the Iron-Handed  
**Title**: Master-at-Arms of the Games Training Grounds (Bilda perimeter)  
**Pronouns**: He/Him  
**Age**: Late 40s  
**Appearance**: Broad-shouldered, thick-bearded, perpetually underslept. Voice like a struck drum. Always in motion when anxious, which is often.

### Personality

Russel is a good man trying to do a nearly impossible job. Loyal to those who give him orders, but not blindly obedient. Protective of his charges. Embarrassed by disorder. Not good at political reading, but deeply intuitive when it comes to physical and emotional pain. Carries a fatherly energy, even when overwhelmed.

- **Disposition**: Earnest, strained, gruff with tenderness underneath  
- **Mannerisms**: Sighs deeply, rubs temples, double-checks instructions. Pauses before committing to praise.  
- **Core Tension**: He wants to serve well, but is unsure what “serving well” means anymore.

### Use in Play

Russel is the anchor for the first arc. He explains the barracks, assigns quarters, and orients the players to the basics of the Games. He is the GM's vehicle for introducing character progression mechanics, social conflict, crafting if players are interested, and early mission framing.

If you have played since the quickstart, now is the time to introduce the archetype mechanics if your group has not yet used this particular mechanic yet.

- Can offer training in **melee combat**, **discipline**, or **unit cohesion**.  
- May request the players to **escort**, **observe**, or **report** on tensions within the camp as per necessary with the group.  
- Can be a moral sounding board or a source of guilty confessions later in the story.

**Stats Template (Narrator Adjust as Needed)**:

- **Tier**: 2 (hardened veteran but past his prime as he’s developed his gut)  
- **Combat**: Moderate (D12+D6 with sword, D8 for defense)  
- **Resolve**: High against intimidation, low against emotional appeals  
- **Influence**: High among trainees, moderate with noble visitors

**Notable Relationships**:

- **The Financier**: Russel answers to them under duress. He does not know the financier’s true motives.  
- **The Child**: He respects the child but fears what they represent. Protective but unsure how to connect.  
- **The Knight**: Dislikes them. Sees them as unhelpful and controlling.

**Secrets (GM Use Only)**:

- Has been asked to “accidentally” delay or misfile candidates before, and has to his unspoken shame.  
- Because of his vulnerability to emotional appeals, he sticks to the insight stance fairly regularly.  
- Has a scrollcase with a scroll and smaller scrollcase inside. Willing to give to the party to help them if asked.

## The Child (Name Withheld)

> "Even a crown is weightless to one who has never lived without it."

**Name**: Unknown (players may only hear it in Gagian)  
**Title**: Ward of the King of Gagia  
**Pronouns**: They/Them (language of origin uses no gendered terms)  
**Age**: 8  
**Appearance**: Clean and ornate tunic, long dark hair, steady posture. Their hands are never idle, always studying or mirroring the adults around them. They do not smile often, but when they do, it is either perfectly rehearsed or surprisingly childish.

### Personality

The child is at once restrained and observant. They speak little due to the language barrier, but communicate volumes through gaze, body language, and routine. Though trained to act with nobility, they exhibit flashes of real emotion—confusion, longing, or defiance—particularly when alone or unguarded. They are the kind of child who would excel if given space, but who has never known space to be theirs.

- **Disposition**: Curious, burdened, quick to imitate authority  
- **Mannerisms**: Folds hands, listens closely, glances to their knight before answering  
- **Core Tension**: They are expected to fulfill a destiny they were never consulted on.

### Use in Play

The child is not a plot device—they are a character. The Narrator should portray them as quietly alive: sometimes overlooked, sometimes overwhelming. Their behavior should evolve based on who the players are and how they treat them.

- Responds to **kindness** with silent attachment.  
- Responds to **dismissal** with disinterest or mimicked coldness.  
- May attempt to **follow** a trusted PC when given the chance.

They understand more than they can express. If the players begin teaching, protecting, or bonding with them, the child will remember—and mimic—their lessons.

**Stats Template (Narrator Adjust as Needed)**:

- **Tier**: 0.5 (not a combatant)  
- **Resolve**: High vs. shame or dismissal; low vs. confusion or conflict  
- **Insight**: High (mirroring and emotional reading), though non-verbal

**Notable Relationships**:

- **The Knight**: Source of structure and protection; obeyed without question.  
- **Russel**: Tolerated, regarded as a functionary.  
- **Players**: Completely neutral until trust is earned.

**Secrets (GM Use Only)**:

- Knows they are royal, but not the true reason they were sent here.  
- Has overheard fragments of the bet but doesn’t understand its full meaning.  
- May choose to speak one word or gesture of significance if they trust a player fully.

## The Knight-Interpreter

> "To be trusted with a future is not the same as being allowed to shape it."

**Name**: Untold; referred to as "Sir" or "the Interpreter" by those at the barracks  
**Title**: Personal Guard and Linguistic Channel for the Ward of Gagia  
**Pronouns**: He/Him  
**Age**: Mid-40s  
**Appearance**: Gaunt, steel-eyed, and quiet. Always armored. Speaks in measured tones with precise diction. Never seen seated.

### Personality

The Knight is a living fortress. Loyal, calculating, and profoundly paranoid. He speaks when needed, and then only exactly what must be said. He rarely translates unless it is necessary for political purposes. Though not cruel, he is uncompromising. He sees all strangers as threats until proven otherwise, and even then, only barely.

- **Disposition**: Stern, watchful, guarded  
- **Mannerisms**: Scans exits, speaks without emotion, places himself between the child and any conversation  
- **Core Tension**: He has orders to protect. To protect one child, he must suspect everyone as a threat.

### Use in Play

The knight is a filter between the child and the world. If the players want access to the child, they must go through him, or find a way around him. Use him as a test of player creativity, empathy, or strategic patience.

- Blocks or redirects most questions aimed at the child.  
- Will refuse duels or challenges but defend lethally if necessary.

**Stats Template (Narrator Adjust as Needed)**:

- **Tier**: 3 (elite bodyguard)  
- **Combat**: High (D12 \+ reroll for initiative, 2d20+d8 on sword, 2d20+d6 on defense)  
- **Resolve**: Very High  
- **Insight**: High (can read intent even in silence)

**Notable Relationships**:

- **The Child**: Protective and emotionally distant. Speaks to them in private but not in front of others so often as possible.  
- **Russel**: Respects the chain of command but distrusts his competence.  
- **Players**: Treats as unpredictable until proven otherwise.

**Secrets (GM Use Only)**:

- Has orders for if the child falls into the wrong hands.  
- Understands the bet but has told no one—not even the child.  
- Has a drinking problem if relevant to the kind of group you're playing with. Otherwise might be underprepared during late-night battles.

# Chapter VI: The Walkthrough (Part 2 — Hervia and Beyond)

> “The closer they draw to the sea, the further they are from who they once were.”

If the first act gave the players control over their pace, this stretch challenges their priorities. What do they carry? Whom do they trust? And what is waiting for them in the world beyond training?

**On the Road**

The players depart the barracks armed with a name, **Hervia,** a harbor city, and a direction, Aber. What Russel did not tell them (because he doesn’t know) is that the roads are increasingly crowded. Not with armies, but with **wealth**. Wealth on the run. Longcugan elites fleeing cities are shifting toward the Bread Standard. Caravans full of textiles, scrolls, mechanical curiosities, and coins disguised as heirlooms. These are not refugees of war. These are refugees of *loss of control*.

Hervia is a small port, having no foreign partners close enough to sail from, it trades locally with the larger, more remote port in Taurion. Taurion has the only real access point to Lonall ports. Lonall does not regularly send ships to Trand, but does have a reputed fleet on patrol for the Trand fleets.

## Encounters Along the Road

Narrators should introduce **three to five optional encounters**, drawn from the characters’ relationships and regional tensions. These are not random. They are echoes of themes introduced earlier: power misused, identity concealed, trust offered or withheld.

Examples include:

- Coming the opposite way, a large caravan with a small family and a lot of guards. They're guarding a lot of wealth but the family acts as if they are poor refugees even as they carry more goods than any family, much of which is extremely valuable.  
- A noble child separated from their retinue, whose behavior eerily mirrors the Gagian ward.  
- A set of scammers looking to bilk refugees by offering to sell them a lot of land that when visited is burned and salted. They try to sell it to multiple refugees.  
- A patrol from the Tratian Empire challenging the players’ right to bear arms or travel unlicensed.  
- Other merchants and so-called refugees from Lonall.

Let players solve these through stance, skill, or silence. The goal is not to test them, but to *watch what they choose* when the script is not obvious and begin seeding the foreshadowing of the second act.

On the River

The fastest route to Hervia is to ride by boat from Bilda to Aldi, but you run into Trakasu along the way. From Aldi to the border check, then by land to Tretidu. Then another hundred miles or so south along the river to Hervia. From there, they can book passage to Taurion. Alternatively, players may take roads the entire way which takes longer, particularly the backroads from Tretidu to Hervia. Finally, players could take a boat down river the entire way but spend some time going a long way around. By boat, the journey is roughly 1,300 miles. By road, approximately 1,100 miles. By the fastest route, approximately 900 miles. The journey should expect to take over a month.

Don't forget to have a scene with the child and the knight to discover their characters deeper.

### Arrival in Trakasu

This city is the capital of Canicia. It is 100 miles south by river or 120 by road, a 3-4 day journey. It contains a fortified palace jutting into the river, the intersection of two major trade routes, and is otherwise a feudal city in its prime. It has no distinguishing features like a library or anything that would stick out. Players could learn more about the Games, the local geography, the path possibilities ahead, and other reasonable information the residents may know and share.

If the players are looking for it, there is rumor of a man in a fence network named Radan that may have insider knowledge about where the crown is held. He is located in Banverver which the players most likely need to pass through regardless.

In the city, there is a performer woman who openly performs in the streets. **Arrival in Hervia**

Hervia is tall, loud, and fragrant with conflicting smells: spice smoke, brine, soot. Its buildings are narrow but many-tiered, with balconies and fabrics waving over footbridges. There are no open forums, instead conversation happens in back rooms and over rooftop drinks.

The players are met, eventually, by a contact. This can vary depending on the Narrator’s style: a courier bearing credentials, a scholar who speaks in metaphors, or a priest who insists on blessings before business. Their job is to confirm passage to Taurion. But something is wrong. There are rumors that **the port is blocked**.

Blockaded, taxed, or under riot, the reason may change. The result is the same: the players must solve, bypass, or wait out the impasse. Here, the child’s presence may provoke unexpected attention. A Gagian heir draws eyes, and the Knight is quick to limit access.

This delay becomes the next opportunity for roleplay, trust-building, and faction engagement. Do the players spend time with Longcugan exiles? Do they align with imperial loyalists? Do they vanish into Hervia’s underside and resurface with contraband?

Between Hervia and Taurion is another couple thousand miles of trade route with a bunch of little town stops and lasting at least a month. Players adept at roleplaying may spend this time as they will. Otherwise, you can use overland travel rules with all waking hours devoted to travel and downtime tasks.

## Taurion

Taurion is bigger than Hervia by a lot. It's very remote, but it is the capital of **Nyria**. Nyria is based on the principles of being a trade partner between Agrumia, Coniaria, Tratian, and the rest of the Otsaiapian Empires. **Money defines their society** the way military might defines the people of Bilda.

The city is a mechanical marvel—**metal cogs and gears everywhere**. Its pathways are winding and vertical, requiring both navigation and adaptation. Some paths dip below ground into mysterious industrial levels. While not fully detailed here, this underground zone offers optional hooks for players interested in exploration or intrigue.

There is a **metal ship**—a sleek, floating vessel—bound for Lonall. It does not belong to Taurion, nor is it part of any known merchant fleet. Players must **secure passage**:

- **Combat**: Seize control of the ship, though consequences are severe.  
- **Diplomacy**: Find translators and arrange payment or offer valuable services.  
- **Diplomatic Letter**: If they brought the scrollcase-within-a-scrollcase from Russel, they are treated as part of a diplomatic envoy. The players must choose who among them will pose as the dignitary, and what roles the rest will serve.

In Taurion, propaganda is widespread. The Bread Standard is referred to as “the foreign experiment” and “an impossible philosophy of the exotic lands.” As a trade hub, Taurion fears disruption of its core power: commerce. Any system that flattens or invalidates monetary hierarchy is treated as a threat.

**Rituals are banned.** Any mention of Lonall rites is met with suspicion or contempt. Players who carry symbols, practice rituals, or associate openly with Longcugan refugees may find themselves watched, followed, or worse.

## Questions Players Might Ask the Interpreter (and How He Might Respond)

**Q: How do people get food here?**  
A: "There are distribution halls, neighborhood by neighborhood. Everyone eats. You go when you're hungry. Bread, fruit, root vegetables, broths, and teas. It's communal, and my family has one of the best cooks in the nation."

**Q: Who owns this house?**  
A: "Could be family-held. If no one lives there, it'll show up on the housing queue. Validators reassign based on need, age, and contribution. Sometimes people trade. Sometimes people just move."

**Q: Is there money?**  
A: "Not the kind you use for food or shelter. We do use credits for luxuries—art, imports, specialty goods. But a lot of trade here is gifting or service exchange. If it feels like you're paying, it's probably something imported."

**Q: What do you do for work?**  
A: "I validate compost health and land readiness. I used to manage earth tumblers. Now I inspect moisture balance, fungal bloom, and community work rotation."

**Q: Can anyone just be anything?**  
A: "You choose your path. Validators support your choice. We offer training, apprenticeships, and correction when needed. But no one's forced to do what they hate."

**Q: Who leads this place?**  
A: "For what? Agriculture? That council is four women and a man that regularly argue about the value of sage. For architecture? That rotates quarterly. There's a governance stream, you can watch every vote cast. but there is a lot of voting so we use techno... magic... aggregators... to summarize and display relevant feeds."

**Q: Are there police?**  
A: "No. We have mediators. And validators. If someone harms another, it goes to the council. If someone disrupts too much, they lose validator access and have to meet the terms of their sentence by a jury."

**Q: How do you enforce laws?**  
A: "We ask what created the harm and who it belongs to. Then we try to fix that. We don’t keep cages here."

**Q: What if someone just wants to take and not give?**  
A: "Then they don't understand and would likely be given a sentence by a council jubilation for civics courses and job selection training and relevant job training until they find how they give. Validators might limit access to luxury cycles or advancement. But no one starves. There is incentive to work, it helps give meaning and access luxury."

**Q: Why is everything so green and open?**  
A: "Because we finally could. Once we stopped building for kings, we built for the soil and the sky."

**Q: What happens if I die here?**  
A: "You're remembered in the soil archive. Named and sung. If you leave something worth holding, we hold it. If you need a ritual, we can offer one."

**Q: Can I join this system?**  
A: "Not today. But you can learn, and you can act as though you belong. That's what makes it real."

**Q: What happens when someone tries to exploit the Bread Standard?**

A: Exploitation doesn’t vanish with better rules, it mutates. Most citizens in Bread-aligned cities are invested in communal well-being and validator accountability.

### Welcome to Aber

**The Park on the Way to the Castle**

As the players make their way toward the castle grounds for the evening event, they must pass through a wide green park that curves alongside the river. It is alive with music, community laughter, and the sounds of a celebration. A child’s birthday party is in full swing—bright pennants strung between trees, a communal table filled with fruits and honeyed breads, and dancers stomping soft rhythms on pressure-sensitive pads that light with color.

At the edge of the gathering, a group of children forms a circle. Led by an elder, they chant a short communal verse:

> “I’m entitled to breathe air.  
> I’m entitled to be fair.  
> I’m entitled to eat, drink, live healthy and be respected.  
> I’m entitled to be treated fair—  
> And to give to others my respect  
> For their air, their fair, their food, and their drink.”

The Interpreter says they also say this ritual in the bars but everyone drinks on drink. The college kids call it the double-shot motto. It took over our pledging of allegiance to the crown and king.

It’s said plainly, not solemnly. But the ritual lingers in the air—part nursery rhyme, part civic oath. Some players may recognize it as a kind of cultural anchoring: a child’s entry into the rights and responsibilities of the Bread Standard.

The party itself is generous but self-organized. Parents serve food in shifts. Teenagers run games under the canopy. The players may be offered a slice of fruitcake or asked to join in a song. If they decline, no one presses. If they linger, someone may ask where they’re headed.

There is no hostility here—only curiosity.

The crown they seek lies not in a vault or throne room, but in a **museum** that these people regard with reverence. It is not owned. It is shared. And from the moment the players pass this park, it becomes clear: this is not a story about reclaiming a king’s prize.

Aber sits at the confluence of several creeks, which converge visibly just northeast of the city proper. Here, the river is born. On either side of this glistening convergence lie visible buildings—community kitchens, ritual halls, validator resource hubs—all accessible by stone or fiber-laced footbridges. The bridges are modest, mostly for people, though one large central bridge is broad enough to allow carriages to pass side by side.

As players proceed a mile into town, something strange and beautiful reveals itself: they’ve been walking over rooftops without realizing it. The homes here are sunken into the ground, camouflaged by walkable, living rooftops of tall grass, flowering moss, and solar-reflective vines. Children play outside, calling out to each other from window hollows. This is not the hidden life of secrecy, but of integration—of ecology made architecture.

The city’s population hovers around 8,000. It is not a capital. It is not a metropolis. It is the first heart of an idea. And it wears that proudly. Older citizens speak with reverence about the change that came when the Bread Standard took root. Younger people simply assume this is the way of the world.

### Districts and Infrastructure

The **public zone** along the river holds most official and semi-official activity: gathering halls, dispute resolution chambers, cooperative shops, health circles, and performance courtyards. Scattered around are **housing complexes** that blend private, partnered, and community living. Multi-family arrangements are common. So are intentionally arranged cohabitations drawn from the community queue compatibility scores.

A **small industrial sector** hums further inland. It’s clean, mostly underground, but its loading areas and warehouse ramps are plainly visible. It serves both the city and the surrounding region.

### Governance and Culture

Governance does not center in one place. Validators work across nodes—some physical, many digital. Decisions are public, process-oriented, and often displayed via park kiosks or community bulletin obelisks. The mayor (formerly king) lives in a standard home, booked the old castle—now a museum—for this event, and walks the streets like anyone else.

His statue, positioned in a riverside park, depicts him sitting casually on a no-armrest chair, three-quarters his actual height. It is unguarded and covered often by flower garlands.

Aber’s rules: welcome the stranger. Aber’s people: not always in sync with that law. But they try.

### Bread Standard Visuals

- **Digital Validator Billboard**: Tucked just behind a row of market stalls along the main riverside path, a large digital mural slowly morphs to display validator decisions, public votes, and project progress. To locals, it’s a tool of accountability. To visitors, it looks like a magical painting that changes faces and colors. It is public, but opaque without translation—and a valuable curiosity for those who ask.  
    
- **Comfort Seating for All**: Public benches use outdoor cushions engineered to shed water and withstand deep cleaning from passing utility carts. They invite lingering—placed near play areas, gardens, and public thought boards. Built for conversation, not control.  
    
- **Underground Access Points**: Subtle ramps and wide staircases with curved metal handrails lead down into light-filled, windowed corridors beneath the town. These public-access tunnels serve as entry points to resource hubs, dispute halls, performance spaces, and rest shelters. None are labeled dramatically—but they are used constantly.  
    
- **Visible Utility Stations**: At the northern edge of town near the creek convergence, a water-harvesting system guides runoff into mossy terraced tanks. At the southern edge, a wastewater plant hums with familiar but softened machinery. Curious players may spot signage or ask locals how it works.

The host wears a finely embroidered mask. When she speaks, her voice resonates with clarity and familiarity, regardless of the language the players speak. After the interpreter tells her they speak Canician, the host adjusts the mask and speaks to the whole party in Canician.

> The woman at the gate wears a mask—not a masquerade disguise, but something more like ceremonial garb. When she speaks, you hear your native tongue. When you respond, she nods, as if hearing hers. The mask must translate, though you see no lights. She touches the side of her hand. A moment later, a projection flares briefly above her hand: names, images, coordinates. It vanishes again.  ‘We’ve been expecting you.’

If shown a scroll from Russell, she will welcome the designated dignitary warmly and gesture to the others as “trusted associates.”

The players are not anonymous here. Their names and roles have been known since the moment they arrived at the outer edge of Lonall. The validator networks predicted their journey, and the social web of this advanced region has quietly ensured their arrival would coincide with this gathering. Their presence was not only permitted—it was prepared for.

Yet they are not the only dignitaries.

The party is a monthly event, attended by validators, storytellers, trade representatives, and curious minds. Most do not care for the imperial politics of Trand—but they care very much about who these strangers are, and why they’ve come.

If the characters have spoken of their intentions in any capacity since arriving in Lonall, then the master of ceremonies will address it to the characters that night during the audience question period.

The hosts of the party, and most of the attendees, are engaged right now in political advertising. They do not treat with appointed political ambassadors, but with the people of their neighboring nations. They do not target with lies and narratives, but with a working show of belief in action. It is highly scripted, from the food to the speaker, to the seating and such, but the voting results are real.

When the characters have had a chance to witness the Bread Standard in action, they will be offered a chance to speak. The host will let them know about the order of the evening and when they will have a chance to speak to their peers, the party, and more. She will guide them into the ballroom, walking past the throne room where the crown is on display.\\

The order of the evening is to dine first, then entertainment, then vote results, then audience question period, then the closing ceremonies when the mayor makes his appearance.

The tables setup before their arrival including the standard loaf at every plate. The interpreter will tell you that it is quite filling and you should save your hunger for the main course. But this bread will be discussed throughout the meal.\\

## At the Table: Moe-Ma and Leanna

As the players are ushered into the dining hall—guided past the crown encased in glass, past the museum's wide hallways repurposed as a gallery of past governance—they arrive at a long, beautifully lit table laid for dozens. Bread loaves wait at every seat. The lighting is warm, low. Conversations drift in polite murmur.

At their assigned seats, the players find two others already present. They rise.

The first woman stands with the graceful poise of someone who has stood for judgment before as an arbiter. Her gown is rich, dyed in deep mineral hues with folds that shimmer in the shifting candlelight. Her fingers close over a small clutch as she inclines her head with solemn charm. Her movements are smooth, polished. Her eyes are observant, not predatory—more like someone filing a mental record for later admiration.

The second woman is younger, in a simple but elegant white tunic-dress that brushes the tops of her sandals. She rises with more motion than grace, excited but composed. Her hands meet in a polite clasp until introductions begin, at which point she instinctively steeples her fingers—a tell of nervous focus.

If the players extend a hand in greeting, both women mirror the gesture without hesitation. Their grips are firm, but not testing. They meet eyes, then names. Their language, should it be unfamiliar, is quickly corrected with the arrival of a server bearing a bundle of translucent scarves. When worn, the scarves allow mutual understanding, a humming resonance carrying one’s words into the listener’s own tongue.

The older woman is known as Moe-Ma. She is not what she seems. She will share later, if asked, or if the conversation drifts toward logistics, that she works in municipal sanitation engineering. Not just a worker, but a validator with significant authority. She and her partner coordinate the entire city’s waste transformation and filtration infrastructure. She speaks with crisp authority, and her language is precise. She does not explain her work in metaphors. To her, a flange is a flange. But she would much rather discuss the mood of the party, the art on the walls, or what relationships among cities have been trending in validator exchanges. If prompted, she may gossip lightly, strategically. Not a villain, but absolutely status-savvy and capable of maneuvering. She is there to gain votes, nothing less or more, but is willing to play along with the political theater she knows this to be.

The younger woman is Leanna. She is in training to become a government functionary—though few call them that in Lonall. Her parents were once burdened by debts so great that they advocated fiercely for the Bread Standard when it was still a theory. She inherited that passion and now serves on several validator councils across legal and civil domains. She writes appeals processes. She tracks precedent. She’s never been part of anything like this dinner, and she’s enchanted by it. Leanna speaks with clarity and an almost childlike eagerness when the topic is law, fairness, or the ways societies might choose each other over fear.

The women do not know each other before this evening, but quickly discover they share acquaintances in the permits department. They respect the moment, however, and do not distract from their primary focus: the dignitaries across from them.

They do not press, but they observe. Moe-Ma measures tone and body language. Leanna occasionally glances at the Interpreter, then back to the party, as if trying to read the whole scene at once.

Neither mentions the crown. Neither treats this like a trap. But both, in different ways, hope the players leave tonight wondering how a world like theirs could be built back home, wherever they come from.

But there is no time to begin speaking to them, the room hushes as the music crescendos and a spotlight (bullseye latern with magically bright light) falls on the master of ceremonies, what is almost certainly a jester. The kind that do not talk during their routine. The kind that does close-up magic, dancing, acrobatics and gymnastics, gestures and is followed by the music.

There are two other open seats at the players’ table which are filled during the dining portion of the evening, while music is loud and the room is fairly dark.

One of these is filled by Rescot Paskerdil, a dignitary from Anabadia, a large nation currently experiencing tense maritime skirmishes with the Otsaiapian Empire. Anabadia claims the large island near the empire’s western coast, a point of ongoing friction. The dignitary is deeply skeptical of the Bread Standard, having attended several prior dinners and left each unconvinced. Despite multiple explanations and good faith efforts by hosts, he remains fixed in his belief that Lonall is hiding something, or staging propaganda. He considers Canicia a potential threat, or perhaps a rival to be courted. His demeanor is wary, his eyes sharp, his words few—but weighty when he chooses to speak.

At the far end of the room, a figure emerges: rainbow-gloved, masked, draped in gleaming ribbons and soft bells. The **Master of Ceremonies** is a mime, a figure of pantomime and performance art—his movements graceful, exaggerated, and silent. He speaks with gesture, posture, and expression.

As he steps forward, the orchestra begins its score in earnest, following his every move as if he were their conductor. A flourish of the hand brings a fan of illusory flowers to life, distributed to guests without ever touching them. A raised eyebrow sends a beam of magical light swirling across the hall to illuminate a person in the crowd, prompting laughter or applause.

The mime directs the rhythm of the evening without saying a thing. Every guest watches. Every guest understands. The spotlight shifts again—to the Interpreter.

The mime halts before him, bows theatrically, and gestures upward as if holding a microphone. The orchestra goes silent.

Now, all eyes turn to the Interpreter. He speaks very loudly in Loncugese and while you don't understand everything, most of what he says is your names to the audience, then a single sentence. (Those that are able to understand, he calls the characters dignitaries from Canicia.) People applaud and he sits, the mime moves the conversation to another table and another speaker.

## A Lull in the Conversation

After the opening statements and the first round of spotlighted speakers, the music softens into something ambient and the meal resumes at a slower pace. As drink refills flow and low murmurs return, the conversation at the table begins again. Let the players spend time talking to the characters at the table. When the conversation lulls too much, introduce the fourth character.

It is during this lull that a fourth member quietly joins the table. He is a man in his forties, with a soft jawline, sun-freckled arms, and the tired cheerfulness of someone who has explained things to children all day and is about to do it again.

He introduces himself simply as Johnathan “Jon” Hinks, a **teacher**—one who teaches **multiple subjects**, mostly to children, with a focus in **civics**. He’s a recurring figure at these dinners and is known to many of the other guests, including the Anabadian dignitary.

As Jon joins, he immediately draws a slight nod from the skeptical visitor. There’s history here—perhaps prior debates. And the teacher leans into it with a casual openness.

> “You again,” the dignitary says, tone wry. The teacher grins. ‘Only so many dinners to go around.’ He leans forward, elbows on the table. ‘You going to let me try the answer again this time?’

Their exchange—measured, patient, sharp—offers the players another window into the cultural ideology of Lonall and the friction it creates for outsiders. This is not the first time the teacher has had to explain what makes the Bread Standard tick. And it won’t be the last.

If the players wish to listen in, they will hear debates around transparency, merit, control, and the feasibility of maintaining such a system. The teacher’s tone never falters.

If players join the conversation, both NPCs will welcome their input, with the teacher occasionally deferring to the Interpreter or asking the players what they think the hardest part of their own governance is.

If you’re looking for different voices, remember Leanna is cheery and optimistic about the Bread Standard, and Moe-Ma has her entire reputation staked in it.

### Conversation and Opening Exchanges

The music dims once again. The spotlight fades. Candles flicker. The atmosphere of the hall shifts back to conversation — intimate, sincere, layered with intent.

This is the moment when players may finally speak freely with their table mates. Moe-Ma and Leanna welcome their questions with grace and curiosity. Moe-Ma remains reserved but generous in her tone. Leanna, bright-eyed and responsive, leans in when addressed, often stealing glances at the Interpreter to gauge nuance.

The Anabadian dignitary listens more than he speaks, cutting in occasionally with terse observations. He remains wary but does not sabotage the discussion. His skepticism, however, is noticeable in every folded arm and sidelong glance.

Let the players shape the tone. If they push for ideology, Moe-Ma responds with pragmatism: cost-per-loaf, validator audits, water management logistics. If they speak of governance, Leanna offers clear descriptions of appeals processes and multi-path votes.

If they ask about the crown, there is a brief pause. Moe-Ma redirects. Leanna furrows her brow. The Anabadian says nothing.

Encourage organic conversation. Plant details. Let the players feel the cultural divide and common ground.

### A Second Spotlight: The Question Period

The music rises again, subtly. The Master of Ceremonies steps back into the center of the room, spotlight returning to his movements. This time, he gestures not to the Interpreter, but to a panel of speakers—validators, artists, public figures—who rise one by one to speak.

These are real-time validator reports. A swirling magical projection appears above the stage, displaying:

Animated charts (voting results, validator weight distributions)

Short clips of debate

Floating text in an unreadable script that occasionally ripples into images or color-coded glyphs

The Interpreter can explain the projection aloud:

"This is a live feed of recent votes in the medical board. You see, the majority of citizens voted against a costly procedure. But the doctor validators overruled them. The voice-weight changed the outcome."

"Two thousand four hundred forty-nine people voted. Sixty-three percent voted no, most of which at a 1:1 vote weight. The cost was high and several actuaries voted no with 3.7 validation weights. The medical circle, one-hundred eleven members with validation weights at over 8.4 per member voted yes. It would seem in this aspect of health, we must override the costs and ensure this care. Their weighting tipped the scale."

This segment is optional. It is dense. But it lets players see:

The tension between direct democracy and weighted validator systems

The transparency of the Bread Standard in action

The value placed on expertise and long-term thinking

Let players ask questions. Let Moe-Ma and Leanna provide answers. Let the teacher weigh in with metaphors that reach for common ground.

### The Mayor Arrives

As the final question fades into the warm hush of reflective chatter, a subtle chime rings out from the edge of the room. The Master of Ceremonies turns, sweeps a low bow, and gestures to a man entering through an arched side passage. A new spotlight illuminates the Mayor of Aber.

He is older, his posture relaxed but proud. His beard is neat, and flour still lingers under his fingernails despite the elegance of his formal tunic. His presence brings a hush, not reverent, but familiar. Everyone here knows who he is. He is pushed in a wheelchair by an attendant.

He smiles softly and speaks:

He speaks of his friendship with Valora, the renowned mind from the Collegium who helped him envision something beyond crowns and decrees, the Bread Standard they're living now.

He does not mention his doubts about the son who once stood to inherit the throne. He does not need to. But if the players end up speaking to him at any length about why, that is certainly part of it. He won’t speak about his son, but will offer to let him visit. His son stays at a residential care facility and spends most of his time coloring the walls there, repainting, and coloring again.

A validator steps forward, mid-fifties, crisp tunic with a matte badge reading: Validator Circle: Fiscal Burdens & Reciprocity.

Her voice is steady, but her words cut clean.

“I want to know,” she says, turning toward the mayor and the audience alike, “why we haven’t narrowed what qualifies under the societal burden. I review these ledgers every cycle. We are sustaining full care access for people who give nothing back. I am asking at what point does dignity become entitlement?”

A ripple moves through the crowd. Some stiffen. Others nod.

The Mayor’s smile fades slightly. He stands.

“Projections show fewer vagrants than expected at this point. Validator rotation programs have already brought us to near-full employment—”

“Three-point-two percent unemployment isn’t everyone,” another voice calls.

The Mayor raises a hand. “Please—only one question at a time. We’ll return to you.”

He turns back toward the first speaker, his tone gentle but firm.

“Our system does not calculate human worth by present utility. And if we falter in supporting the least among us, we train the next generation to measure themselves by ledger lines instead of shared time together.”

A pause. No applause. Just listening.

As he speaks, the Master of Ceremonies begins to move again, this time toward the players. The spotlight follows.

"And now, honored guests... are you still here to steal the empty crown?"

"Why are you supporting a Gagian in obtaining the emperorhood?"

The hall stills.

This is the cliffhanger. The reveal. The moment of reckoning. How the remainder of the night resolves is up to the party.
